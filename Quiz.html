A simplified map of the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) and the central connections that help stabilize gaze and posture.

Horizontal VOR: head turns right

Eye movement is equal, opposite, and compensatory to head movement.

Push–pull coding

Turning the head right increases right canal firing and relatively decreases left canal firing.

Paired eye movement

Abducens motor neurons drive the left lateral rectus; internuclear fibers ascend in the MLF to the right medial rectus.

Beyond the three-neuron arc

Vestibular nuclei connect to several systems.

Vestibular nuclei ↔ cerebellum

Flocculus, nodulus, and related regions help calibrate VOR gain and integrate vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive signals.

Ascending ocular motor pathways

Projections to CN III, IV, and VI nuclei coordinate gaze; the MLF links abducens and oculomotor nuclei for horizontal gaze.

Descending vestibulospinal pathways

Lateral and medial vestibulospinal tracts contribute to postural control, head stabilization, and balance responses.

Clinical localization

Central ocular motor signs can arise from brainstem or cerebellar lesions; interpret findings with the full syndrome and examination.

Nystagmus & ocular motor localization

Read the slow phase, quick phase, gaze dependence, and associated signs together. These are localization clues, not stand-alone diagnoses.

Peripheral vestibular tone imbalance

Example: acute unilateral loss on the left.

Why it beats: a unilateral reduction in resting vestibular input creates an asymmetric tonic signal. The brain interprets that imbalance as motion; the slow drift is the vestibular imbalance, and resetting saccades make the quick phases.

Alexander’s law: in typical spontaneous peripheral vestibular nystagmus, intensity increases when looking in the direction of the fast phase (here, right gaze). This is a pattern, not a rule that proves a peripheral cause; central gaze-evoked nystagmus and mixed disorders can complicate interpretation.

Direction is named by the fast phase. Fixation may damp some peripheral nystagmus; failure of fixation suppression can suggest central/cerebellar dysfunction, but neither observation should be used alone.

Horizontal saccades & gaze holding

Fast gaze shifts need a pulse; maintaining eccentric gaze needs a neural integrator.

PPRF: a horizontal saccade generator in the pons drives ipsilateral gaze through the abducens nucleus. A destructive lesion often impairs saccades toward the lesion, but acute pontine lesions can also affect vestibular and internuclear pathways.

Horizontal gaze holding: nucleus prepositus hypoglossi (NPH) and medial vestibular nucleus contribute to the neural integrator. A leaky integrator lets the eye drift back toward center, producing gaze-evoked nystagmus.

Vertical saccades & gaze holding

Vertical and torsional fast eye movements localize to the rostral midbrain network.

riMLF: the rostral interstitial nucleus of the MLF contains burst neurons for vertical and torsional saccades. Lesions may cause slowed or hypometric vertical saccades; patterns depend on lesion extent.

INC and posterior commissure: the interstitial nucleus of Cajal helps hold vertical/torsional gaze. Posterior commissure involvement classically disrupts upgaze pathways, but vertical gaze palsy has multiple possible causes.

Slow eye movements: what do they test?

Separate pursuit, VOR, and gaze holding before localizing a deficit.

Smooth pursuit

Tracks a moving visual target. Distributed cortical visual-motion networks (including MT/MST and frontal eye fields) project through pontine relays and cerebellum to ocular motor/vestibular pathways. Pursuit is age-, attention-, and speed-dependent; asymmetry can support but rarely uniquely localizes.

VOR slow phase

Stabilizes gaze during head movement. Peripheral labyrinth/nerve, vestibular nuclei, ocular motor nuclei, and cerebellar calibration contribute. An impaired VOR can be peripheral or central; interpret alongside head impulse, nystagmus, and other signs.

Gaze holding

Neural integrators convert a brief saccadic command into sustained eccentric eye position. Horizontal: NPH and medial vestibular nucleus. Vertical/torsional: interstitial nucleus of Cajal. Integrator dysfunction gives centripetal drift, often with corrective gaze-evoked nystagmus.

Ocular motor localization clues

FindingStructures to consider
Impaired horizontal saccadesPPRF, abducens nucleus, MLF, or supranuclear inputs; distinguish conjugate gaze palsy from internuclear ophthalmoplegia.
Impaired vertical saccadesriMLF and rostral midbrain networks; assess up- and downgaze separately.
Gaze-evoked nystagmusNeural integrator, cerebellum, drugs, or other central causes; clinical context matters.
Impaired pursuitDistributed visual, cortical, pontine, and cerebellar networks; not a single-site localizer.
A bedside ocular motor sign is not a diagnosis. Consider visual acuity, attention, age, medications, direction and velocity of the slow phase, and the rest of the neurologic examination.
